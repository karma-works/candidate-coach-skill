# Interview Strategy Criteria

Use this reference to shape `application-strategy.md`, review application materials, and prepare a candidate for interviews. It contains strategy criteria, not candidate evidence.

## Source note

This is an original, compact synthesis of broadly useful interview-preparation concepts, informed in part by Shel Leanne's *How to Interview Like a Top MBA*. It does not reproduce the book's prose, endorsements, anecdotes, worksheets, sample answers, or question catalogue. The candidate's knowledge base remains the only source for personal claims.

## The core standard

An effective application should make one coherent case: the candidate understands the opportunity, can connect relevant evidence to it, and can discuss limitations without distortion. Optimize for credibility and relevance, not a flawless persona.

Define before drafting:

- the first impression the employer should retain;
- two or three role-relevant themes supported by evidence;
- the strongest example behind each theme;
- the most likely concern and the honest response to it;
- why this role, company, and timing make sense based on supported facts.

## Prepare in four areas

### Role

- Extract outcomes, responsibilities, seniority, constraints, and success measures from the job description.
- Distinguish essential requirements from preferences and decorative wording.
- Determine whether the employer needs a builder, operator, specialist, change leader, or a blend.
- Anticipate what strong performance would look like in the first year.

### Company

- Research products, customers, business model, current priorities, culture signals, and relevant recent developments from reliable sources.
- Identify a small number of company-specific reasons for interest. Avoid generic admiration.
- Note uncertainties that should become interview questions rather than assumptions.

### Industry

- Understand the market forces, vocabulary, competitors, customer problems, and material risks that shape the role.
- Prepare to translate the candidate's experience into the employer's business context.

### Interviewers

- When interviewer information is public and professionally relevant, understand their function and likely perspective.
- Use this only to improve relevance and rapport. Do not infer sensitive traits or manufacture familiarity.

## Build an evidence map

For every major requirement, label the candidate's support as:

- `strong`: direct, specific evidence at a comparable level;
- `adjacent`: transferable evidence with a meaningful difference;
- `gap`: no adequate support in the knowledge base.

For each strong or adjacent match, capture the situation, candidate action, decision or tradeoff, result, and relevance to the target role. Keep team outcomes distinct from individual contributions.

Use the evidence map to select examples. Do not force every experience into the application, and do not convert proximity into equivalence.

## Shape a clear career narrative

The CV and interview should explain the logic of the candidate's progression without rewriting history.

- Connect moves through increasing scope, deeper expertise, changing interests, or deliberate transitions when evidence supports that story.
- Explain short tenures, employment gaps, layoffs, or career changes directly and briefly.
- Keep the explanation factual, non-defensive, and forward-looking.
- Show what the candidate learned or did during a gap only when the knowledge base supports it.
- For a nontraditional background, name the difference, translate the truly transferable capabilities, and acknowledge the domain learning still required.

## Answer structure

Coach concise answers with four parts:

1. `Point`: answer the question immediately.
2. `Evidence`: give one concrete example with the candidate's actual role.
3. `Outcome`: state the supported result or learning.
4. `Relevance`: connect the example to the opportunity.

Not every answer needs equal detail. Keep simple factual questions brief. Use fuller evidence for leadership, judgment, conflict, failure, change, and delivery questions.

Open-ended prompts are opportunities to reinforce the chosen themes, but the answer must still address the question asked. Do not turn every response into a sales pitch.

## Question families to prepare

### Introduction and résumé walk-through

Prepare a short professional arc that prioritizes the most relevant work, explains transitions, and ends with why the current opportunity is a logical next step.

### Motivation and fit

Connect supported candidate preferences with specific role and company facts. Explain both what the candidate hopes to contribute and what makes the environment suitable. Avoid prestige, compensation, or generic enthusiasm as the sole rationale.

### Achievements and impact

Choose examples that demonstrate the target capabilities. Clarify the problem, constraints, personal contribution, decision-making, and result. Do not borrow a team's entire outcome as an individual claim.

### Leadership and teamwork

Prepare examples covering direction-setting, delegation, influence without authority, coaching, conflict, stakeholder alignment, and difficult decisions. Match the claimed leadership scope to the evidence.

### Problems, failures, and weaknesses

Choose a real but discussable example. State what happened without blaming others, show ownership proportional to the candidate's role, explain the corrective action, and identify the durable learning. Never disguise a strength as a weakness or invent a harmless failure.

### Ethics and judgment

Use an example that shows how the candidate identified competing obligations, gathered relevant input, made a principled decision, and managed consequences. Preserve confidentiality and avoid presenting illegal or unethical conduct as cleverness.

### Career goals

Give a direction that is believable from the candidate's record and compatible with the opportunity. Do not promise a tenure, promotion, or destination the candidate cannot honestly support.

### Gaps and concerns

Address the concern directly, then distinguish what is already supported, what is transferable, and what must be learned. A credible development plan is stronger than denial or overclaiming.

## Delivery and interaction

- Make the opening minutes calm, prepared, and role-aware.
- Listen to the complete question and answer it before adding context.
- Use concrete nouns and verbs; avoid vague self-praise and rehearsed slogans.
- Balance concision with enough detail to make the evidence verifiable.
- Adjust depth to the interviewer while keeping the underlying facts stable.
- Treat informal conversations as part of the evaluation without becoming artificial.
- Practice aloud and revise answers that sound memorized, evasive, or longer than their value warrants.

## Questions for the employer

Prepare questions that reveal how the role really works and help the candidate make a decision:

- What outcomes matter most in the first six to twelve months?
- Why is the role open, and what should the new hire preserve or change?
- Where do priorities, resources, or stakeholder expectations currently conflict?
- How are decisions made and success measured?
- What distinguishes people who thrive on this team?
- What concern, if any, remains about the candidate's fit?

Do not ask for facts that basic preparation should have uncovered. Timing-sensitive topics such as compensation or benefits should follow the context and stage of the process rather than a rigid prohibition.

## Closing and follow-up

At the end of the interview:

- summarize interest in one or two specific sentences;
- connect the strongest evidence theme to the employer's stated need;
- clarify next steps and timing;
- invite unresolved concerns when appropriate.

Afterward, record new facts, commitments, names, open questions, and any claim that needs verification. Send a brief, personalized follow-up when appropriate. It should show attention to the conversation, reinforce a genuine fit point, and avoid pressure or repetition.

## Application-strategy output

Translate these criteria into a concise strategy with:

1. target role and employer context;
2. intended first impression;
3. two or three winning themes;
4. evidence map: strong, adjacent, and gaps;
5. likely concerns and honest response lines;
6. company, industry, role, and interviewer research still needed;
7. interview examples to prepare;
8. useful employer questions;
9. follow-up themes;
10. application-process or AI-policy risks.

Keep the strategy internal. Do not paste its labels, gap analysis, or coaching commentary into the candidate-facing CV or cover letter.
